🧠 RNN vs Transformer

Two ways a computer can read human language — explained in easy English, with drawings.

← Back to Learning Path
Big picture: An RNN reads a sentence one word at a time and keeps a small memory. A Transformer looks at all words at once and lets every word pay attention to every other word. See also: RNNs & LSTMs and Transformers.

1. RNN — Reads one word at a time 🚶

An RNN reads a sentence like a person walking down a line of words. It reads one word, remembers a little, then reads the next word.

Think of a chain: each word passes a small note (its memory) to the next word.

👍 Good: simple, reads in natural order.
👎 Problem: slow, and it forgets words that are far away.

2. Transformer — Looks at all words together 👀

A Transformer does not walk word by word. It puts all words on the table and lets every word look at every other word at the same time. This "looking" is called attention.

👍 Good: very fast (reads all at once) and never forgets far-away words.
👎 Cost: needs more computer power for long text.

3. A simple example 📝

"The keys on the table ... are missing."

To choose "are" (not "is"), the model must remember the word "keys" from far back.

🚶 RNN: may forget "keys" because it is far away.
👀 Transformer: looks straight back at "keys" — easy!

5. Is Transformer the winner? 🏆

For human language (chatbots, translation, writing) — yes! 🎉

Every big AI model today is a Transformer:

GPT · Claude · Gemini · Llama · DeepSeek · Qwen

Since about 2018–2020, almost no one builds a new big language model with RNN.

6. Is RNN dead (deprecated)? ⚰️❌

No! RNN is not dead. It just lost the spotlight 💡.

RNN (and LSTM, GRU) is still:

  • ✅ Fully supported in PyTorch, TensorFlow, Keras
  • ✅ Taught in every AI class as a base idea
  • ✅ Used in real products — see below 👇

7. Who still uses RNN now? 🔧

RNN is good when data comes as a stream, or the device is small and cheap.

WhereWhy RNN still fits
⌚ Tiny devices
(watches, sensors)		RNN is small and light; a Transformer is too heavy
🎙️ Live streaming
(audio, typing next word)		RNN reads "one new item at a time" very naturally
📈 Short time-series
(ECG, stock ticks, weather)		Sequences are short — RNN is cheap and good enough
🗣️ Some speech systemsLow delay (low latency) for real-time results
📉 Small dataTransformers need lots of data; a small RNN can win

8. A surprise: RNN is coming back! 🔄

Transformers have one weak point: cost grows very fast for long text (every word checks every other word = length × length).

So researchers built new RNN-style models — fast like RNN, smart like Transformer:

  • 🐍 Mamba (State Space Models) — streams long text cheaply
  • 🦅 RWKV — "an RNN that trains like a Transformer"
  • 🔁 xLSTM (2024) — a modern, upgraded LSTM

These are not the old RNNs — but they bring back the key idea: keep a running memory instead of looking at everything at once.

9. Remember this one line ⭐

🚶 RNN = read word by word, keep a small memory.

👀 Transformer = see all words at once, connect any word to any word.

🏆 For big language models, Transformer is the winner today.

⚰️❌ But RNN is NOT dead — it is a light tool for streams and tiny devices.

🔄 And the RNN idea is coming back with Mamba, RWKV, and xLSTM.

10. Real world: live captions & interpreting 🎧

Question: when Zoom or YouTube show live captions or live translation, do they use RNN or Transformer?

Short answer: mostly Transformer today — but RNN-style models still survive in one part (streaming speech recognition). To see why, you have to know that "real-time interpreting" is not one model. It is a pipeline of 2–3 steps:

Step 1 — Speech-to-Text (ASR): the one place RNNs survive

Turning sound into words is the step where RNN-style models are still genuinely used in production. A design called the RNN-Transducer (RNN-T) was built specifically for streaming: it can emit words while you are still talking, with very low delay. Google used RNN-T for on-device speech. Because low latency matters so much for live captions, RNN-T stuck around longer here than anywhere else.

That said, newer systems lean on Transformer-based speech models — for example Whisper (Transformer) and Conformer (convolution + Transformer), often as a Conformer-Transducer that keeps the streaming transducer idea but swaps the RNN core for a Transformer core. So even here, the direction is toward Transformers.

Step 2 — Translation (MT): fully Transformer

Translating the recognized text into another language is now entirely Transformer territory. Google Translate used stacked LSTMs around 2016 (the "GNMT" system), but switched to Transformers a few years later. Every major translation system today is Transformer-based. This is the clearest "Transformer won" part of the pipeline.

Step 3 — Text-to-Speech (only if you want a spoken voice)

If the output is a spoken voice (true "interpreting," not just captions), the system also needs text-to-speech. This too moved away from RNN-based models (like the older Tacotron) toward Transformer and diffusion models for more natural voices.

But wait — isn't RNN supposed to be better at streaming?

Good instinct. RNNs process one piece at a time, so they feel like a natural fit for live audio. Transformers normally look at a whole sequence at once, which sounds wrong for real-time. The trick: in practice, streaming systems feed the Transformer a short rolling window — a few hundred milliseconds of audio at a time — instead of the whole conversation. This "chunked" approach gives near-real-time output while keeping the Transformer's accuracy. Combined with being more robust to accents and background noise, that is why Transformers took over even this live use case.

So, Zoom and YouTube specifically?

These companies don't fully publish their production stacks, so this part is informed inference, not confirmed fact:

  • YouTube auto-captions & auto-translate run on Google's modern speech and translation stacks — Transformer-based translation, with Transformer (and likely some RNN-T) streaming speech recognition.
  • Zoom live captions and translated captions use modern Transformer-based models (Zoom built up translation capability including the Kites team); live translated captions are a Transformer-era feature.

Takeaway: Live interpreting on video platforms is mostly a Transformer job now. The only place RNN-style models still genuinely live is streaming speech recognition (RNN-Transducer), because it was designed for low-latency, word-by-word output — and even that is slowly becoming Transformer-based. This is a perfect example of the big rule: RNNs survive in streaming, low-latency niches; Transformers own the heavy language work.
