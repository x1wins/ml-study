1. What a logarithm is

A logarithm is simply the inverse of raising a number to a power.

The one idea to remember

If a base b raised to the power y gives x, then the logarithm base b of x is y. Exponentiation and logarithms are two ways of writing the same relationship:

by = x  ⇔  logb(x) = y

Read logb(x) out loud as: "the power you raise b to, to get x." For example, since 23 = 8, we have log2(8) = 3.

Exponent formLog formIn words
102 = 100log10(100) = 210 to the power 2 is 100
210 = 1024log2(1024) = 102 to the power 10 is 1024
e1 = 2.718…ln(2.718…) = 1e to the power 1 is e
b0 = 1logb(1) = 0anything to the power 0 is 1
Why it matters: Exponentials grow explosively; logarithms undo that growth. If a quantity multiplies, its logarithm just adds — and addition is much easier and more numerically stable to work with.

Exponential and log are mirror images across y = x

Because a log undoes an exponential, their graphs are reflections of each other across the diagonal line y = x. Where the exponential shoots up, the log rises slowly.

Key intuition: the exponential passes through (0, 1); its mirror, the log, passes through (1, 0). The log grows without bound but ever more slowly — that slow growth is exactly what lets it compress huge ranges.

2. Common bases: 10, 2, and natural log

A log always has a base. Three bases dominate in practice.

The three logs you will meet constantly

BaseNotationAnswers the questionWhere it shows up
10 log10(x) or log(x) How many powers of 10? decibels, pH, "orders of magnitude", log-scale plots
2 log2(x) How many doublings? information / bits, entropy, algorithm complexity
e ≈ 2.718 ln(x) (natural log) How many e-foldings? calculus, gradients, log-likelihood, cross-entropy
Why is e "natural"? Because the exponential ex is its own derivative, the natural log has the cleanest calculus of any base — which is why ML almost always uses ln. See Euler's number (e) for why e is special.

Handy exact values: log10(1000) = 3, log2(8) = 3, ln(1) = 0, and ln(e) = 1.

All three on one graph

Same shape, different steepness. Every log of base > 1 passes through (1, 0) and is just a vertical rescaling of the others (logb(x) = ln(x) / ln(b)). Smaller base → steeper curve.

Labeled dots mark every whole-number (x, y) point in view. A log reaches an integer height y at x = basey, so the neat points are: log₂ at (2,1), (4,2), (8,3), (16,4), (32,5), (64,6); log₁₀ at (10,1) and (100,2); and all three share (1, 0). ln only lands on (1, 0) exactly — because e is irrational, it never hits another integer x at an integer height. Notice how much faster log₂ climbs than log₁₀.

3. The log rules

A handful of identities turn hard multiplications and powers into easy additions.

The four rules that matter

RuleFormulaPlain meaning
Product log(a · b) = log(a) + log(b) a product becomes a sum
Quotient log(a ÷ b) = log(a) − log(b) a ratio becomes a difference
Power log(an) = n · log(a) an exponent comes down as a multiplier
Change of base logb(x) = ln(x) ÷ ln(b) convert any base into natural log
logb(a · b) = logb(a) + logb(b)   ·   logb(an) = n · logb(a)
The product rule is the star of the show in ML. When you multiply thousands of tiny probabilities together the result underflows to zero. Take the log and each factor becomes a term you simply add — no underflow, and gradients stay well-behaved.

Rules in one worked example

Say you need log10(50). Write 50 as 100 ÷ 2 and apply the quotient rule:

log10(50) = log10(100) − log10(2) = 2 − 0.301 = 1.699

Every messy computation with logs reduces to these add / subtract / multiply moves.

4. Why logs are useful

Three superpowers

  • Turn multiplication into addition — the product rule replaces a long product with a sum, which computers add without losing precision.
  • Compress huge ranges (log scale) — numbers from 1 to 1,000,000 span just 0 to 6 on a base-10 log axis, so tiny and enormous values fit on one chart.
  • Handle very small probabilities — a probability of 10−300 underflows to 0.0 in floating point, but its log (−690.8) is a perfectly ordinary number.
Why it matters in ML: loss values, learning rates, and gradients often span many orders of magnitude. Plotting or searching them on a log scale makes patterns visible and makes optimization stable. See Math Foundations for the broader toolkit.

Example 1 — multiplication becomes addition

Say you need 1000 × 100000. With logs (base 10) you just add the exponents:

log(1000) + log(100000) = 3 + 5 = 8 → answer = 108 = 100,000,000

This is exactly how slide rules worked, and why the product rule log(a·b) = log a + log b is so handy: a hard multiplication turns into an easy addition.

In ML: the probability of a whole sentence is the product of each word's probability, e.g. 0.2 × 0.1 × 0.05 × …. Multiplying hundreds of small numbers is slow and unstable; adding their logs is fast and safe.

Example 2 — compressing a huge range (log scale)

Imagine plotting these five values on a normal axis:

value        1     100    10,000   1,000,000
log10(value) 0      2       4          6

On a linear axis, 1 and 100 would be squashed invisibly against zero while 1,000,000 dominates. On a log scale they become evenly spaced (0, 2, 4, 6) — every ×100 step is the same visual distance, so you can see all of them at once.

In ML: this is why learning-rate sweeps use log spacing (0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1) and why loss curves are often plotted on a log axis — early training drops loss by orders of magnitude, and a log scale keeps the later fine detail visible.

Example 3 — rescuing tiny probabilities from underflow

Multiply 400 word-probabilities of about 0.01 each. The true product is 10−800 — far smaller than the smallest number a 64-bit float can hold (≈ 10−308), so the computer stores it as exactly 0.0. All information is lost.

product of 400 probs of 0.01   → 0.0        (underflow: useless)
sum of 400 × log(0.01)          → −1842.07   (perfectly fine)

Working in "log space" (adding logs instead of multiplying probabilities) keeps the number in a normal, comparable range. To recover a probability you exponentiate at the very end.

log P = Σ log(pᵢ) → P = e(log P)
In ML: this is the whole reason log-likelihood and cross-entropy are computed with logs — see Loss Functions. It also connects to Euler's number e, since ln is log base e.

Example 4 — log scales you already use in real life

Many everyday scales are logarithmic precisely because they cover enormous ranges. Each unit is a fixed multiplier, not a fixed amount:

ScaleEach step meansSo …
Decibels (sound)+10 dB = ×10 power70 dB is 100× the power of 50 dB
Richter (earthquakes)+1 = ×10 amplitudea magnitude 6 is 100× a magnitude 4
pH (acidity)−1 pH = ×10 more acidicpH 3 is 1000× more acidic than pH 6

The takeaway: on a log scale, equal spacing = equal ratios. That's the same reason a loss curve dropping from 10 → 1 → 0.1 looks like even steps on a log axis.

Example 5 — log-odds (logits): logs power classification

A probability p lives in a cramped range (0 to 1). Its log-odds (the "logit") stretches it to the full number line, which is far easier for a model to work with:

logit(p) = ln( p / (1 − p) )

Worked values:

p = 0.5   → ln(0.5/0.5)   = ln(1)  =  0      (50/50, neutral)
p = 0.9   → ln(0.9/0.1)   = ln(9)  = +2.197  (confident yes)
p = 0.1   → ln(0.1/0.9)   = ln(1/9)= −2.197  (confident no)

Notice the symmetry: p and 1−p give opposite-sign logits. The sigmoid function is exactly the inverse — it maps a logit back to a probability:

σ(x) = 1 / (1 + e−x) (undoes the logit)
In ML: neural-network classifiers output raw logits; sigmoid or softmax turn them into probabilities, and cross-entropy (built on ln) scores them. Logs and the exponential ex are two sides of the same coin — see Neural Networks and Euler's number e.

5. The derivative of ln(x)

One of the cleanest results in calculus

The slope of the natural log at any point x is just 1 / x:

d/dx · ln(x) = 1 / x

And by the chain rule, the log of a function has a slope of that function's derivative divided by the function itself:

d/dx · ln(f(x)) = f′(x) / f(x)
Why it matters: because ln has such a simple derivative, taking the log of a loss keeps the gradient math clean — the backbone of gradient descent. If you need a refresher on slopes and the chain rule, see Derivatives.

6. Where logs appear in ML

Logs are not a side topic in machine learning — they are load-bearing.

Log-likelihood: products → sums

Models often assign a probability to each data point, and the likelihood of the whole dataset is the product of all of them. Multiplying thousands of numbers below 1 collapses to zero. Taking the log converts that product into a sum:

log ∏i pi = Σi log(pi)

We maximize the log-likelihood (equivalently, minimize the negative log-likelihood) because it is numerically safe and easier to differentiate.

Cross-entropy / log loss

The standard classification loss is built directly from logs. For a true distribution y and predicted probabilities ŷ:

CE = − Σi yi · log(ŷi)

For binary classification this becomes:

BCE = −[ y · log(ŷ) + (1 − y) · log(1 − ŷ) ]
Intuition: if the model puts high probability on the correct class, log(ŷ) is close to 0 (small loss). If it confidently predicts the wrong class, log(ŷ) plunges toward −∞ (huge penalty). Dig deeper on the Loss Functions page.

Logits and log scales

  • Logits — the raw pre-softmax scores of a network. The name literally comes from "log-odds": logit = log( p ÷ (1 − p) ).
  • Log-scale hyperparameter sweeps — learning rates are searched across orders of magnitude (0.1, 0.01, 0.001…), so we sample them uniformly in log space rather than linear space.

7. Runnable code (numpy)

Log turns a product into a sum

import numpy as np

a, b = 7.0, 3.0

# The product rule: log(a * b) == log(a) + log(b)
lhs = np.log(a * b)          # log of the product
rhs = np.log(a) + np.log(b)  # sum of the logs

print(lhs)                   # 3.044522437723423
print(rhs)                   # 3.044522437723423
print(np.isclose(lhs, rhs))  # True

# Scaled up: multiplying 1000 tiny probabilities underflows to 0.0,
# but summing their logs stays finite.
probs = np.full(1000, 0.01)          # 1000 probabilities of 0.01
print(np.prod(probs))                # 0.0  (underflow!)
print(np.sum(np.log(probs)))         # -4605.17...  (perfectly fine)
Takeaway: np.prod underflows to 0.0, destroying the information; np.sum(np.log(...)) preserves it. This is exactly why ML works in log space.

Cross-entropy computed with logs

import numpy as np

# True label: class 1 (one-hot over 3 classes)
y_true = np.array([0.0, 1.0, 0.0])

# Model's predicted probabilities (must sum to 1)
y_pred = np.array([0.1, 0.7, 0.2])

# Cross-entropy = -sum( y_true * log(y_pred) )
# A tiny epsilon avoids log(0) = -inf if a prediction is exactly 0.
eps = 1e-12
ce = -np.sum(y_true * np.log(y_pred + eps))

print(round(float(ce), 5))   # 0.35667  == -log(0.7)

# Sanity check: because y_true is one-hot, CE is just -log(prob of correct class)
print(round(float(-np.log(y_pred[1])), 5))  # 0.35667

The loss equals −log(0.7) ≈ 0.357. Push the correct-class probability toward 1 and the loss falls toward 0; let it drop toward 0 and the loss explodes.

Quick recap

  • Logarithm — the inverse of exponentiation: by = x means logb(x) = y
  • Bases — 10 (orders of magnitude), 2 (bits), e (natural log, used in ML)
  • Rules — product → sum, quotient → difference, power → multiplier
  • Derivative — d/dx ln(x) = 1 / x, the cleanest slope in calculus
  • In ML — log-likelihood, cross-entropy / log loss, logits, log-scale sweeps
