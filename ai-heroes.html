A very short history of AI 🕰️

AI has gone through boom-and-bust cycles. The "deep learning" era we live in now took decades to arrive.

1950 — Alan Turing asks "Can machines think?" and proposes the Turing Test.
1956 — The Dartmouth workshop coins the term "Artificial Intelligence." Big optimism begins.
1958 — Rosenblatt's Perceptron: the first trainable artificial neuron.
1969 — Minsky & Papert show the perceptron's limits → first "AI winter" (funding dries up).
1986 — Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams popularize backpropagation, reviving neural nets.
1989–98 — LeCun builds LeNet, an early CNN that reads handwritten digits (used on bank checks).
1997 — Hochreiter & Schmidhuber invent the LSTM (an RNN with long memory).
2003 — Bengio and colleagues publish the neural language model → the seed of word embeddings.
2011 — Andrew Ng co-founds Google Brain; the "Google cat" learns from unlabeled YouTube frames.
2012 — AlexNet (Krizhevsky, Sutskever & Hinton) crushes ImageNet → the modern deep learning boom.
2017 — Google's Transformer ("Attention Is All You Need") replaces RNNs for language.
2018 — Bengio, Hinton & LeCun win the ACM Turing Award ("Nobel Prize of Computing").
2022→ — ChatGPT and modern LLMs bring AI to the mainstream.

Each bold algorithm above has its own guide on this site — follow the links in the profiles below.

The people behind it 🧑‍🔬

Bengio, Hinton, and LeCun jointly won the 2018 Turing Award and are often called the "Godfathers of AI." A popular (unofficial) fourth name is Andrew Ng, for bringing AI to the world through Google Brain and online education. Here is what each is famous for — and what they say about where AI is heading.

1. Geoffrey Hinton

— "The Godfather of Deep Learning"

Core work: Backpropagation & AlexNet

Hinton co-popularized backpropagation (Rumelhart, Hinton & Williams, 1986) — the method that lets a network learn by correcting its own errors, layer by layer. In 2012, his students Alex Krizhevsky and Ilya Sutskever built AlexNet, a deep CNN that dominated the ImageNet contest and single-handedly kicked off the modern deep learning revolution.

Recent view: existential risk ⚠️

In 2023 he left Google to speak freely about AI danger. He argues AI is advancing faster than he expected and may soon surpass human intelligence. His worry: digital minds can copy and share what they learn instantly — something biological brains cannot — which could let AI eventually out-compete or manipulate humanity.

On this site: Neural Networks (backprop) · CNNs (AlexNet) · Gradient Descent

2. Yoshua Bengio

— pioneer of neural language models

Core work: word embeddings & neural language models

Bengio's landmark contribution is the neural probabilistic language model (2003), which represents words as high-dimensional vectors (word embeddings) so a network can capture meaning and context. This idea is foundational to how modern systems like ChatGPT understand language. His group also advanced attention for machine translation, a direct ancestor of the Transformer.

Recent view: safety & regulation 🛡️

Bengio warns that advanced AI could slip out of human control or be misused (cyberattacks, bio-weapons). He signed the 2023 letter calling to pause giant AI experiments and now pushes for strong government regulation and legally binding safety rules before powerful models ship.

On this site: RNNs & LSTMs · Transformers (attention) · Embeddings & RAG

3. Yann LeCun

— "The Father of CNNs"

Core work: Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN)

LeCun created the CNN, a network specialized for images that learns visual features in local patches. His LeNet read handwritten digits on bank checks in the 1990s. CNNs now power autonomous driving, medical image analysis, and face recognition.

Recent view: optimistic, skeptical of LLMs 🤔

LeCun argues today's LLMs are limited — they mostly predict the next word and lack real understanding, planning, or common sense. He pushes a shift toward "objective-driven" / world-model AI that can observe and plan like humans. He is optimistic and dismisses doomsday scenarios.

On this site: CNNs · Neural Networks · RNN vs Transformer

4. Andrew Ng

— the popular "4th": AI's great teacher

Core work: Google Brain & democratizing AI

Ng was the founding lead of Google Brain (the famous "Google cat" result, where a huge network learned to spot cats from unlabeled YouTube frames). He co-founded Coursera and DeepLearning.AI, teaching AI to 8M+ learners, and was Chief Scientist at Baidu. More than a single algorithm, his impact is making ML practical and accessible to everyone.

Recent view: practical optimism 🚀

Ng is famously optimistic. He calls fears of AI extinction overblown and compares worrying about it to "worrying about overpopulation on Mars." His focus: applying AI to real problems and building a skilled workforce. (Note: not an official Turing-trio member — included as the widely cited fourth figure.)

Why they disagree: all four built the field, yet they split on risk — from Hinton's alarm to LeCun's and Ng's optimism, with Bengio in between pushing guardrails. That honest disagreement among the pioneers is itself worth remembering: no one knows for sure where AI goes next.
