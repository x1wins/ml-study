℮ Euler's Number e: The Base of Natural Growth

Where e = 2.71828… comes from, why its own derivative is itself, and why it makes almost every AI formula cleaner — explained simply, with runnable code.

1. What is e?

A universal constant, just like π — but instead of circles, it describes growth.

A constant, like π

Everyone knows π = 3.14159… is the number that shows up whenever you deal with circles. e = 2.71828… is the number that shows up whenever something grows or shrinks continuously — money earning interest, populations, radioactive decay, or the confidence scores inside a neural network.

  • e is irrational — its digits go on forever with no repeating pattern
  • It is the base of natural growth (and of the natural logarithm ln)
  • First few digits: 2.718281828459045…
Why it matters: You do not memorize e — you understand what it does. It is the growth rate that "compounds on itself" as smoothly as mathematically possible.

2. Where e comes from — compound interest

The classic story: imagine a magical bank paying 100% interest per year.

The 100% interest thought experiment

You deposit $1. The bank promises 100% interest per year. The question is: how often do they pay it?

Step by step: compounding twice

If interest is paid once at year end, you simply double: $1 → $2.00. But what if they split it into two 50% payments?

$1 × (1 + ½) = $1.50   →   $1.50 × (1 + ½) = $2.25

The second half-payment earns interest on the interest from the first half. That extra $0.25 is the "interest on interest." Compounding more often earns more — but how much more?

The compounding table

Split the year into n equal periods, each paying 1/n interest. Your final balance is (1 + 1/n)n:

Compounds per year (n)FormulaFinal balance
1 (yearly)(1 + 1/1)12.000000
2 (half-yearly)(1 + 1/2)22.250000
4 (quarterly)(1 + 1/4)42.441406
12 (monthly)(1 + 1/12)122.613035
365 (daily)(1 + 1/365)3652.714567
∞ (continuous)(1 + 1/n)n as n → ∞2.718281… = e
The punchline: compounding infinitely often does not make you infinitely rich. The balance climbs toward one specific number and stops there. That number is e.

3. The limit definition

e as a limit

Written formally, e is what (1 + 1/n)n approaches as n grows without bound:

e = limn → ∞ (1 + 1/n)n = 2.718281…

The curve shoots up early, then flattens — each extra compounding step adds less and less, pressing the balance against the dashed line at e.

4. Why it converges — and never reaches 3

Diminishing extra gains

Look at the jumps in the table: 2.00 → 2.25 → 2.44 → 2.61 → 2.71. Each step forward is smaller than the one before:

  • 1 → 2 compounds: gain of +0.25
  • 2 → 4 compounds: gain of +0.19
  • 4 → 12 compounds: gain of +0.17
  • 12 → 365 compounds: gain of +0.10

The gains shrink fast enough that the whole sum is capped. No matter how often you compound — every second, every nanosecond — the balance can never pass 2.71828…. It gets arbitrarily close to e but never reaches 3.

Why it matters: "Infinite compounding" sounds explosive, but it settles on a finite, well-behaved number. That stability is exactly why e is safe to build formulas on.

5. The magic property: its own derivative

ex is the only function whose slope equals itself

The derivative of a function is its slope — how fast it is changing. For almost every function the slope is a different function. But for ex, the slope at every point is exactly the height of the curve there:

d/dx  ex  =  ex

So at x where the curve is 5 units tall, it is also rising at 5 units per step. The bigger it gets, the faster it grows — growth feeding on itself. That is the very definition of "natural" growth.

Why it matters: Training a neural network is all about derivatives (gradients). A function that is its own derivative makes those gradients trivial to compute — no messy chain of new terms appears. See derivatives.html for the calculus behind it.

6. Where e shows up in AI

Once you look for it, e is everywhere in machine learning.

The greatest hits

  • Sigmoid — squashes any number into 0–1 for probabilities: σ(x) = 1 / (1 + e−x)
  • Softmax — turns a list of scores into probabilities that sum to 1: exi / Σj exj
  • Cross-entropy loss — the standard classification loss, built on the natural log (the inverse of e): −ln(ŷ)
  • Normal (Gaussian) distribution — the bell curve behind weight initialization and noise: e−x²/2
  • Learning-rate decay — smoothly shrink the step size over time: lr₀ × e−decay·t
The pattern: whenever AI needs a smooth curve that turns raw numbers into probabilities — or a growth/decay that behaves predictably under calculus — e is the natural choice. See neural-networks.html and transformers.html for these in action.

7. Worked softmax example

From raw scores to probabilities

Suppose a model outputs three raw scores (logits): [2.0, 1.0, 0.1]. Softmax exponentiates each with e, then divides by the total so they sum to 1.

softmax(xi) = exi / (ex1 + ex2 + ex3)

Step 1 — exponentiate each score:

  • e2.0 ≈ 7.389
  • e1.0 ≈ 2.718
  • e0.1 ≈ 1.105

Step 2 — add them: 7.389 + 2.718 + 1.105 = 11.212.

Step 3 — divide each by the total:

Scoreescore÷ totalProbability
2.07.3897.389 / 11.2120.659
1.02.7182.718 / 11.2120.242
0.11.1051.105 / 11.2120.099

The three probabilities sum to 1.000. The biggest score gets the biggest share — but every class keeps a nonzero probability, which is exactly what we want.

Runnable code — compute e and softmax

import numpy as np

# 1) Approaching e via compound interest: (1 + 1/n)**n
for n in [1, 2, 4, 12, 365, 1_000_000]:
    approx = (1 + 1 / n) ** n
    print(f"n = {n:>9,}  ->  {approx:.6f}")

print("numpy's e     ->", np.e)   # 2.718281828459045

# 2) Softmax turns scores into probabilities using e
def softmax(x):
    e = np.exp(x - np.max(x))     # subtract max for numerical stability
    return e / e.sum()

scores = np.array([2.0, 1.0, 0.1])
probs  = softmax(scores)
print("softmax        ->", np.round(probs, 3))  # [0.659 0.242 0.099]
print("sums to        ->", probs.sum())         # 1.0

Expected output: the compound values climb 2.0 → 2.25 → 2.441 → 2.613 → 2.715 → 2.71828…, and the softmax prints [0.659 0.242 0.099].

8. Why e makes AI math clean

Two clean derivatives

Training is repeated differentiation, so we want functions whose derivatives are simple. e gives us the two cleanest ones in all of calculus:

  • d/dx ex = ex — the exponential is its own slope, so gradients through ex add no extra clutter
  • d/dx ln(x) = 1/x — the natural log's derivative is just 1/x, far tidier than logs of any other base

Because sigmoid, softmax, and cross-entropy are all built from ex and ln, their gradients collapse into short, elegant expressions. For example, the derivative of the sigmoid is simply:

σ'(x) = σ(x) · (1 − σ(x))
Bottom line: a different base would force an ugly constant factor (a ln term) into every gradient. Choosing e makes those factors equal to 1 and the math disappears — which is why the whole field standardized on it. Build your foundations in math-foundations.html.

Quick recap

  • e = 2.71828… — an irrational constant, the base of natural growth
  • Origin — the limit of (1 + 1/n)n from continuous compounding
  • Magic — ex is its own derivative
  • In AI — sigmoid, softmax, cross-entropy, Gaussian, learning-rate decay
  • Why chosen — cleanest possible derivatives (ex and 1/x)
