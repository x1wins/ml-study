∫ Integration: Adding Up the Infinitely Small

Antiderivatives, definite integrals, the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, and area under a curve — explained simply, with the "why it matters" and runnable code.

← Back to Learning Path
Big picture: Integration is the process of adding up infinitely many tiny pieces to measure a whole — usually the area under a curve. The result of that process is called an integral. And here is the punchline: integration is the exact inverse of differentiation. One takes things apart into rates of change; the other adds those rates back up.

1. The Intuition — accumulation & area

Integration answers one question: "If I add up all the little bits, how much do I have in total?"

Adding up infinitely many slivers

Imagine a curve f(x) drawn above the x-axis. The area trapped between the curve and the axis is hard to measure directly — it is a weird curved shape. The trick of integration is to slice that area into skinny rectangles, add up their areas, and make the slices infinitely thin. The sum becomes exact.

area ≈ Σ f(xi) · Δx  →  ∫ab f(x) dx

The stretched-out ∫ symbol is literally an old-style "S" for sum. The dx is the width of each infinitely thin slice.

Why it matters: "Total distance from a speed graph", "total probability under a bell curve", "total charge from a current" — anytime you accumulate a changing quantity over an interval, you are integrating.

2. Process vs. Result — and the inverse of the derivative

Two words that are easy to mix up

TermWhat it isExample
IntegrationThe process (the verb) — the act of finding the area / antiderivative"Integrate 2x."
IntegralThe result (the noun) — the answer you get"The integral is x² + C."
The key relationship: integration is the inverse of differentiation. If differentiating x² gives 2x, then integrating 2x gives back x² (plus a constant). They undo each other — see derivatives.html for the forward direction.

Differentiation ↔ Integration

3. The Indefinite Integral (antiderivative + C)

"What function, when differentiated, gives me this?" — the answer is the antiderivative.

Finding the antiderivative

An indefinite integral has no start/end points. It asks for the whole family of functions whose derivative is f(x). It is written with the ∫ sign and no limits:

∫ 2x dx = x² + C

Why the + C? Because the derivative of a constant is zero. So x², x² + 5, and x² − 100 all have the same derivative 2x. We cannot know which constant it was, so we tack on an unknown constant C.

Why it matters: The indefinite integral gives a formula you can reuse. The definite integral (next section) plugs numbers into that formula to get an actual area.

4. The Definite Integral (area from a to b)

Add the two limits a and b, and the integral becomes a single number: the area.

A number, not a function

A definite integral has a lower limit a and an upper limit b. It equals the signed area under f(x) between those points:

∫ab f(x) dx = area from a to b

You compute it by finding the antiderivative F(x), then subtracting its value at the two endpoints (the constant C cancels out, which is why definite integrals give a clean number):

∫ab f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a)

Example — area under 2x from 0 to 3, using F(x) = x²:

∫03 2x dx = F(3) − F(0) = 3² − 0² = 9
Signed area: area below the x-axis counts as negative. So an integral can come out to zero even when the curve is far from flat — the parts above and below can cancel.

5. The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

The bridge that connects the two great ideas of calculus — and proves they are inverses.

Integrating a derivative recovers the original

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC) says the two operations are two sides of one coin. In plain words: if you integrate the rate of change of something, you get back the total change of that something.

∫ab F′(x) dx = F(b) − F(a)

Here F′(x) is the derivative of F. Integrating it from a to b hands back the net change in F. Differentiation and integration cancel:

d/dx ∫ax f(t) dt = f(x)
Why it matters: This is what lets us compute areas without summing infinitely many rectangles by hand. Find an antiderivative, plug in the endpoints, subtract. It also explains why "the integral of a speed graph is the total distance travelled" — speed is the derivative of position.

6. Common Integration Rules

A small toolbox handles most integrals you will meet early on.

The rules you use most

RuleFormulaExample
Power rule ∫ xn dx = xn+1 / (n+1) + C  (n ≠ −1) ∫ x² dx = x³/3 + C
Constant multiple ∫ k·f(x) dx = k ∫ f(x) dx ∫ 5x dx = 5x²/2 + C
Sum rule ∫ [f + g] dx = ∫ f dx + ∫ g dx ∫ (x + 1) dx = x²/2 + x + C
Constant ∫ k dx = kx + C ∫ 7 dx = 7x + C
Reciprocal (special!) ∫ 1/x dx = ln|x| + C the power rule breaks at n = −1
Exponential ∫ ex dx = ex + C its own antiderivative!
Note the special case: the power rule needs to divide by n+1. If n = −1 that is division by zero — so ∫ 1/x dx is not a power; it is ln|x| + C. And ex is famously its own derivative and its own integral — see euler-number.html for why e is so special.

7. Numerical Integration (when there is no neat formula)

Most real-world functions have no tidy antiderivative — so we approximate the area numerically.

Riemann sums & the trapezoidal rule

A Riemann sum is the original idea made literal: chop the interval into rectangles and add their areas. A trapezoidal rule is smarter — it uses trapezoids (slanted tops) that hug the curve better, so it needs fewer slices for the same accuracy.

Why it matters: Computers cannot do symbolic calculus by default — they add up numbers. Numerical integration is how libraries evaluate probabilities, expected values, and losses over data. NumPy ships np.trapz for exactly this.

Approximate ∫01 x² dx (true value = 1/3)

We know analytically that ∫01 x² dx = x³/3 evaluated from 0 to 1 = 1/3 ≈ 0.3333. Let us confirm that numerically with the trapezoidal rule:

import numpy as np

# The function we want the area under
def f(x):
    return x ** 2          # ** is Python's exponent operator

# 1) Slice [0, 1] into many points
x = np.linspace(0, 1, 1001)   # 1001 points => 1000 trapezoids
y = f(x)

# 2a) Built-in trapezoidal rule
#     NumPy 2.0 renamed np.trapz -> np.trapezoid; support both:
trapezoid = getattr(np, "trapezoid", getattr(np, "trapz", None))
area_builtin = trapezoid(y, x)

# 2b) Trapezoidal rule written out by hand (same idea)
dx = x[1] - x[0]
area_manual = dx * (y[0] / 2 + y[1:-1].sum() + y[-1] / 2)

print(f"built-in trapz: {area_builtin:.6f}")   # 0.333333...
print(f"manual trapz  : {area_manual:.6f}")    # 0.333333...
print(f"exact (1/3)   : {1/3:.6f}")            # 0.333333

# A crude left Riemann sum for comparison (less accurate)
riemann = (f(x[:-1]) * dx).sum()
print(f"left Riemann  : {riemann:.6f}")        # ~0.3328

Both trapezoid estimates land on 0.333333 — matching the exact answer 1/3 to six decimals. The cruder Riemann sum is close but slightly off, which is why the trapezoidal rule is preferred.

8. Where Integration Shows Up in ML & Stats

Integration is the quiet engine behind probability, expectation, and model evaluation.

Area under a probability density = probability

A continuous random variable is described by a probability density function p(x). You cannot read a probability off it directly — you must integrate over a range. The area under the curve between two values is the probability of landing in that range:

P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = ∫ab p(x) dx

And because some value must occur, the total area under any valid density is exactly 1:

∫−∞∞ p(x) dx = 1
The normal (bell) curve: the famous Gaussian is normalized precisely so its total area equals 1. That normalizing constant 1/√(2πσ²) is chosen to make the integral come out to one.

Expected value = integral of x weighted by its probability

The expected value (mean) of a continuous variable is the balance point of its distribution — each value x weighted by how likely it is:

E[x] = ∫ x · p(x) dx

More generally, the expectation of any function g(x) is E[g(x)] = ∫ g(x) · p(x) dx. Expectations sit at the heart of loss functions, variance, and information theory. See math-foundations.html for the broader math toolkit.

AUC — the area under the ROC curve

When you evaluate a classifier, the ROC curve plots the true-positive rate against the false-positive rate. The single-number summary AUC (Area Under the Curve) is — as the name says — the integral of that curve:

AUC = ∫01 TPR(FPR) d(FPR)

An AUC of 1.0 is a perfect classifier; 0.5 is random guessing. In practice it is computed with the trapezoidal rule over the ROC points — exactly the numerical integration from section 7. Full detail lives in evaluation-metrics.html.

Diffusion & continuous-time models (briefly)

Modern generative diffusion models (and neural ODEs) are described by continuous processes. Sampling from them means integrating a differential equation over time — numerically stepping from noise to a finished image by accumulating many tiny updates. The same "add up infinitely small pieces" idea, now driving state-of-the-art image and audio generation.

Takeaway: from a single probability to a generated image, integration is how we turn a rate or a density into a total.

Quick recap

  • Integration — the process of adding up infinitely small pieces (area under a curve)
  • Integral — the result of that process (a function, or a number)
  • Indefinite integral — the antiderivative, always with + C
  • Definite integral — a number, computed as F(b) − F(a)
  • FTC — differentiation and integration are inverses
  • In ML — probabilities, expected values, AUC, and diffusion models all rely on it
